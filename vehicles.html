Titling a vehicle in your business's name

There are two different paths, and they don't share a checklist: moving a car you already own into the LLC, or having the LLC buy a car new. Here's what each one actually involves.

PATH ONE

Transferring a vehicle you already own

This is a retitling, not a purchase — the DMV treats it as a change of ownership from you to your company.

Gather the paperwork

States vary, but you'll typically need a bill of sale, the current title, your LLC's articles of organization, and proof of insurance before you go anywhere.

File the title transfer

At the DMV, you'll complete a title transfer form moving ownership from you to the LLC. Some states require the form to be notarized to confirm you're authorized to sign on behalf of both parties.

Pay the transfer fees

Expect a title transfer fee plus anything state-specific — a new plate, for instance, if you want one issued to the LLC.

Wait on the new title

The replacement title, listing the LLC as owner, arrives by mail. It can take a few weeks, and it's the only document that makes the change official.

Still financing the car? You'll need your lender's sign-off before the title can move. Not every lender allows it — some require you to refinance the loan through the LLC first, using a new business loan to pay off the personal one.

PATH TWO

Buying a vehicle directly through the LLC

Here the LLC is the buyer from day one, which changes what a dealership or lender asks for.

Open a business bank account first

The purchase needs to run through the LLC's own account, not yours. Confirm the LLC is fully registered in your state before you shop.

Shop with the commercial department

Many dealerships have a separate commercial or fleet sales desk. Calling ahead saves a trip, and it's worth comparing more than one dealer on price.

Line up financing before you sign

A newly formed LLC often has no credit history of its own, so a lender may ask for your personal guarantee or personal credit history as backup. Established entities with a season of revenue have more room to negotiate.

Register in the LLC's name

Bring the LLC's articles of organization, EIN, and proof of insurance to registration. The title and registration both list the business, not you, as owner.

Insurance works differently

A vehicle titled to an LLC generally needs a commercial auto policy rather than a personal one. Not every insurer writes commercial coverage for every vehicle type, and requirements shift by state and by how the vehicle is actually used — so this is one call worth making before you finalize a purchase, not after.

Regular personal auto coverage can deny a claim outright if it turns out the car was being used for business, which is the scenario this whole structure is meant to avoid creating by accident.

The LLC doesn't cover the driver. If you're behind the wheel and cause an accident, you can still be personally sued alongside the LLC — the business shield protects the company's assets, not the individual actions of whoever is driving.

TAX SIDE

What actually affects the deduction

Titling the car to the LLC doesn't automatically create a deduction — business use does. Ownership just makes the paperwork simpler to keep straight.

FactorWhy it matters
Business-use percentageOnly the share of driving that's actually for the business is deductible — commuting and personal errands don't count, regardless of whose name is on the title.
Vehicle weight & sizeDepreciation and expensing rules apply differently to heavier vehicles versus standard passenger cars.
Mileage vs. actual expenseYou generally pick one method per vehicle — a standard mileage rate, or actual costs like fuel, insurance, and depreciation — and the better choice depends on the vehicle and how it's used.
Records keptA dedicated business account and a mileage log make the deduction easy to substantiate if it's ever questioned. This is where LLC ownership genuinely helps.
