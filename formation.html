FORMATION GUIDE

Setting up the entity before you buy anything

Every asset guide on this site assumes these seven things already exist. Skip one, and a dealer, lender, or title company will usually catch it and stop the deal.

Choose a state and an entity type

Most owners form the LLC in their home state, or the state where the asset will primarily be used. Filing fees and member-privacy rules on the public formation record vary widely by state, so it's worth a quick comparison before you file.

File the formation paperwork

Filing articles of organization with the state creates the entity. You can file directly with your state's Secretary of State (or equivalent office), through a formation service, or through an attorney — the document you get back is your LLC's equivalent of a birth certificate, and banks and lenders will ask for it.

Get an EIN

An Employer Identification Number is the LLC's tax ID with the IRS — effectively its own Social Security number. It's free and available directly from the IRS, and it's required on every loan application, bank form, and dealership finance form tied to the business.

Write an operating agreement

This document spells out who owns the LLC and how it's run. Some states don't require one on file, but lenders and title companies frequently ask to see it, and it's the document that backs up your authority to sign on the entity's behalf.

Set up a registered agent and business address

Every state requires a registered agent — a person or service authorized to receive legal documents for the LLC. A dedicated business address and phone number also make the entity look established to lenders, separate from wherever you personally live.

Open a dedicated business bank account

Bring your articles of organization, EIN confirmation, and personal ID to a bank or credit union. From this point on, every purchase-related payment — down payments, loan payments, insurance, fuel, maintenance — runs through this account and nothing else.

Give it a little history before you buy

A few months of real activity — invoices, deposits, vendor payments — shows a lender the entity is active, not brand new with nothing behind it. Then you're ready to make the purchase itself.

Keeping the entity alive

Forming the LLC is the start, not the end. Most states require an annual or biennial report and fee to keep the entity in good standing. Miss one, and the state can administratively dissolve the LLC — which quietly erases the liability protection you formed it for in the first place, often without you noticing until something goes wrong.

A simple calendar reminder for your state's renewal date does the job. Formation services that offer compliance alerts are solving exactly this problem.

Federal reporting update. As of August 2026, U.S.-formed LLCs and their owners are no longer required to file beneficial ownership information with FinCEN under the Corporate Transparency Act — that federal reporting requirement was permanently rolled back for domestic entities. A separate, narrower rule does still apply to certain cash real estate purchases; see the real estate guide.
