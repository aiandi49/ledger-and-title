What an LLC actually protects — and what it doesn't

The asset guides on this site cover the mechanics. This page covers the limits, because most of the value of the structure disappears the moment those limits get ignored.

LIABILITY, HONESTLY

The corporate veil is a real thing, and it can be pierced

An LLC separates the business's liabilities from your personal ones — but only as long as you actually treat it like a separate entity.

Commingling funds

Paying personal bills from the business account, or vice versa, is the most common reason courts disregard the LLC and hold the owner personally liable anyway.

Personal guarantees

If a lender required your personal signature on a loan, that debt follows you regardless of whose name is on the title. The LLC didn't remove your liability on that specific loan — it just added a second party who also owes it.

Your own actions

The LLC shields the business's assets, not a person's own negligence. If you personally cause harm — driving a company car, for instance — you can still be named and held liable individually, alongside the LLC.

FREQUENTLY ASKED

Questions worth answering before you form anything

Can a brand-new LLC get financing without my personal guarantee?

Rarely at first. Most lenders want to see one to two years of financial history and revenue before extending credit based on the business alone. Until then, expect to personally guarantee most loans, meaning the debt follows you even though the LLC is the named borrower.

Does putting an asset in an LLC hide it from a lawsuit or bankruptcy?

Not if the transfer happens after the debt, lawsuit, or obligation already exists. Courts can undo a transfer made to dodge an existing creditor under fraudulent transfer laws, and bankruptcy trustees specifically look for exactly this pattern. This structure is about how you title a purchase you're about to make — not a way to move existing assets out of reach of people you already owe.

Do I still owe personal income tax on LLC income?

Usually yes. Most small LLCs are taxed as pass-through entities, meaning profits and losses flow through to the owner's personal tax return whether or not the LLC also owns the car or the house. An LLC changes ownership and liability, not necessarily who reports the income.

Is an "anonymous LLC" actually anonymous?

Partially, and less than it used to be. A handful of states don't list member or manager names on the public formation record, which keeps casual public searches from turning up your name. But your identity is still collected by your bank when you open an account, and — since March 2026 — certain non-financed residential real estate purchases made through an entity trigger a federal report identifying the individuals behind it. "Anonymous" limits who can find your name casually; it doesn't remove you from every official record.

Do I need different insurance once an asset is titled to the LLC?

Almost always. Personal auto and homeowner's policies are underwritten for individual ownership and personal use, and can deny a claim outright if the asset turns out to be titled to a business. Commercial auto, landlord, or general business policies are built for entity ownership and business use instead.

Can I move my current daily driver into the LLC even though I still drive it myself?

Only if it's genuinely used for business purposes, and states generally expect that to be true, not just claimed. If a vehicle is titled to the LLC but used entirely for personal errands, that's exactly the kind of mismatch between paper and reality that can undermine the liability protection and complicate the tax position.

Does this require me to be a large business already?

No. Every guide on this site describes a single-member LLC formed specifically to hold one purchase — a common, legal structure for sole proprietors, freelancers, and small business owners, not just large companies.

This is not legal, tax, or financial advice

Everything on this site is general educational information about how LLC ownership commonly works for titling vehicles, real estate, and other purchases. Rules vary by state, by lender, and by your specific situation, and they change — this guide was last checked against current sources in September 2026. Before forming an entity or moving a purchase through one, talk to a licensed attorney, accountant, or tax professional about your specific circumstances.
