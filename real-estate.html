ASSET GUIDE · REAL ESTATE

Holding property title in an entity's name

Buying a home, rental, or other property through an LLC changes how you close, how you finance, and — as of a 2026 federal rule — what gets reported when you pay cash.

TWO WAYS TO CLOSE

Cash versus a commercial loan

The route you take determines both the paperwork and how much of your personal credit history gets pulled into the deal.

Paying cash

The LLC is the buyer of record on the purchase agreement. Funds move by wire or cashier's check straight from the LLC's bank account, and the deed is issued in the LLC's name only.

No personal credit check is involved in the purchase itself, since there's no loan underwriting to do.

Financing the purchase

Residential mortgages are underwritten for individual borrowers, so an LLC purchase generally needs a commercial or portfolio loan instead. Expect higher interest rates and larger down payments — often in the 20–30% range — than a standard residential mortgage.

A newly formed LLC with no financial history will usually need a personal guarantee from you until it has its own track record.

2026 UPDATE

Cash purchases through an entity now get reported

As of March 1, 2026, FinCEN's Residential Real Estate Reporting Rule requires certain non-financed residential purchases made through a legal entity or trust to be reported to the federal government.

What this actually covers. If your LLC buys a 1–4 unit home, condo, co-op, or residential land without traditional lender financing, the closing or title company is required to file a report identifying the individuals who own or control the buying entity.

You aren't the one filing it — that responsibility sits with the settlement agent — but expect to be asked for beneficial-ownership information and identification as a routine part of closing on or after that date. This applies nationwide, not just in the handful of cities the older, narrower version of this rule used to cover.

In other words: an entity purchase still separates the property from your name on the public deed, but it no longer keeps the purchase itself invisible to federal regulators the way an all-cash LLC deal once could.

FORMATION CHOICES

Where people form the holding entity

Some owners form the purchasing LLC in a different state from where the property sits, usually for the state's own LLC laws rather than to hide anything about the transaction itself.

  • Match the state to the property, or not. You can form an LLC in one state and register it to do business in the state where the property is located (a "foreign LLC" registration) — this adds a filing but is routine.
  • Member privacy on the formation documents varies by state: some states list LLC members and managers on the public formation record, others don't. That's separate from federal beneficial-ownership reporting, which now applies mainly to non-financed entity purchases as described above.
  • One property per entity is a common practice for rental portfolios, so a liability issue at one property doesn't reach the others.

What you give up

Holding a primary residence in an LLC generally forfeits the personal-residence capital gains exclusion available to individual owners, and it can complicate homeowner's insurance, homestead protections, and certain refinancing options that are built around individual ownership.

A "due-on-sale" clause in an existing mortgage can also be triggered by transferring a property you already own into an LLC, which is why this is usually done at the time of purchase rather than after the fact.

Form & register the entity

Before you make an offer, not after.

Line up financing or cash

Commercial lenders want to see the entity and, often, your guarantee.

Close in the LLC's name

The purchase agreement and deed both name the entity as buyer.

Insure it as a business asset

A landlord or commercial policy, not a standard homeowner's policy.
